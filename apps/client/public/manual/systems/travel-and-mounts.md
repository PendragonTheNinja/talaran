A hundred kilometres, end to end. That is Taiar Island, and I want you to sit with the number a moment, because it is the single most underestimated fact about the place.

You will spend more of your life on the roads than in any workshop, at any outcrop, in any field. New arrivals treat travel as the dull bit between the interesting bits. Then they look up one evening and realise the dull bit *was* the evening.

So let us make the roads cheap. Everything on this page is a world rule and will follow you to whatever islands Talaran opens next. Only the hundred kilometres belongs to Taiar.

## Two ways to cross, one skill to learn

Everything about travel divides along one line. Are you on your own feet, or on something else's? Happily, the answer no longer decides what you learn. Both roads teach **Wayfaring**.

**On foot**, every level of Wayfaring takes a little more off your journeys. Permanently. Everywhere. The early levels give the most, and the later ones keep giving, ever less, for as long as you keep at it.

**Mounted**, the beast has done the heavy work before your skill is consulted at all. Each mount has a pace it starts at and a pace it can be coaxed down to, and Wayfaring closes the gap between the two. The rouncey, for one:

## The floor

Now the important part, and the part that saves people from a doomed ambition.

**A journey can never fall below a tenth of its base time.** Not with the finest mount in Talaran, not with a lifetime of Wayfaring, not with every scrap of travel gear ever forged. One tenth, and no further.

I have met men who set out to break that floor as a life's work. I liked them enormously. They were wrong, and their journeys were exactly as long as everyone else's.

The floor exists so that distance means something. A world you can cross instantly is not a world, it is a menu.

## Gear that helps on foot

Certain equipment trims your journeys directly: a good staff in your hands, decent boots on your feet. These add together, and they add on top of Wayfaring.

Your starting **Lanai Staff** does this. It is a modest three percent, and I would forgive you for dismissing it, but three percent of every journey you will ever take is not a modest number at all. It is a modest-*looking* number, and those are the ones worth checking twice.

## Experience is paid on the honest distance

Here is my favourite thing about travel in Talaran, and I have thought about it more than is healthy.

**You are paid for how long the journey would have taken, not how long it took you.**

Get faster and you lose nothing. The road that once cost you an hour still pays as though it cost you an hour, forever. Every improvement you make is pure profit, and the world never claws it back.

I find this quietly moral. Most systems in most worlds punish you for getting good at them.

One wrinkle. Riding pays at a lower rate than walking, so the same road earns rather less Wayfaring from the saddle. Consider it the price of comfort. You are, after all, sitting down.

Walkers see more, too. A rider passes the odd thing in the verge at a trot. A walker stops and picks it up.

## What I would actually do

Ride when you need to be somewhere. Walk when you don't.

That is most of it. A journey on horseback is over sooner, and the time it saves goes back to the bench or the rock face. A journey on foot pays better for the same road and turns up the odd find besides. Both feed the same skill, so no trip is wasted and nobody need agonise over which way to go.

And travel is passive. Set out, then go and do something else while the road happens. A man watching his own journey progress is a man who has read none of my other pages.
