Let us begin with a confession: I was a dreadful farmer.

Oh, I *understood* farming. I understood it the way a man falling off a cliff understands gravity, which is to say thoroughly and far too late to be useful. I had come from the forests, where patience means swinging until the tree gives up, and from the forges, where patience means waiting for metal to stop being so angry. Soil, I assumed, would be a third variety of the same virtue.

Soil does not care what you assume. Soil has one lesson and it delivers it to everyone identically: *you cannot hurry this, and you should go away.*

## Where and how to start

Farming begins with property, which surprises people who expected it to begin with a seed.

You do not sow a field on common ground. You raise a **farmstead** first, and the fields come after. On Taiar, that means **Novita**, and it means speaking to **Georgic**, who teaches the whole sequence in the proper order. That is considerably more than I had, and he does it without once mentioning that you might have asked sooner.

Raising the farmstead is a serious piece of joinery: five hundred planks, five hundred blocks of granite, a thousand nails, and the better part of ten minutes' honest work. Note that it pays **Carpentry** experience rather than Farming, and pays it generously. I have always thought that a fair acknowledgement of what it costs a man. Just don't forget a mallet and saw.

On **Antura**, the homestead ground is **Farefield**. Every island has a homestead of its own, raised the same way and at the same price, and each is a separate piece of ground with its own fields, pens, and store. Owning one on Taiar gives you nothing on Antura but the knowledge of how it is done, which is worth more than you would think.

## The work, in order

A field turns the same wheel every time, on any island you ever farm:

1. **Enclose** it, with fencing and a stone border. Three minutes.
2. **Till** the soil, ninety seconds, the only step that strictly demands a hoe in your hands.
3. **Sow** your seed, a few seconds each, ten seeds to a full field.
4. **Wait.** The crop grows in real time, whether you are present or not.
5. **Harvest**, a touch slower than sowing, and the step that finally pays you.

Between step four and step five you should be somewhere else entirely. That is not a suggestion for the efficient. That is the design.

## Tools and gear

- **Hoe, must be equipped.** In your hands, to till. Owning one accomplishes nothing. A Serph hoe is the proper tool for Antura's fields; an Ambren one still tills them, slower.
- **Mallet, must be equipped.** In your hands, to raise a farmstead or fence a field.
- **Saw, carried.** It need only be in your pack when you build.
- **Bucket, carried.** It need only be in your pack when you tend.

## Soil, and the great argument about it

Soil sits in one of three conditions, depleted, ordinary, or rich, and it governs **how much you harvest and nothing else whatsoever**.

Your experience does not move. The work was the same work; only the field's generosity differed. I have heard men argue otherwise at length and with feeling. They were wrong at length and with feeling.

A hungry crop drags the soil down a step when you pull it. Legumes lift it, being the only genuinely polite plants. A field left alone recovers on its own, roughly a step every eighteen hours. Manure lifts it at once, if you have any and can bear to part with it.

Rich soil is worth about a quarter again over ordinary. Depleted costs you nearly half. Neither is worth a moment's panic.

Pull up a strawberry bed or a raspberry row and the soil pays for it after all. Roots that have held a field for seasons tear it as they come out, and it drops a step.

## What a field teaches

Your first field teaches you the whole of what its crop is worth. The second teaches a little less, and each one after a little less again, though never nothing. The farm screen marks each field with its share.

This is not meanness. A man with twenty fields does not learn twenty times what a man with one does; he learns to keep twenty fields, which is a smaller lesson than it sounds. The digging, the sowing, and the harvest are paid in full on every field, and the crop is never smaller. More fields are more food, more seed, and more experience. Just not twenty times more.

## Every soil grows its own

A seed knows where it came from. **Taiar's crops take root only in Taiar's soil, and Antura's only in Antura's.** Carry a carrot seed to Farefield and it will sit in your hand and decline.

Antura's six begin at **Farming 13**: **Barley**, **Cabbage**, **Leek**, **Beet**, **Broad Beans**, and **Gooseberry**. They are slower than Taiar's, a day or more each, and pay for it. Broad Beans mend the soil as peas do. Gooseberry is a bush, and like the strawberry it stays in the ground and fruits again.

Their seeds are not sold by anyone respectable. You find them. The **Seed Ground** at Farefield gives up all six, to a forager of 13 or better, and the strandline on Antura's islets washes up a few more.

**Barley** comes out of the field as barley, not sheaves, and wants no threshing. Mill it at your homestead and three of it make two of flour, a better bargain than grain's. Bakers and brewers will both be glad of you.

## More than one homestead

Antura's homestead holds at most **ten fields**, not Taiar's twenty. New ground is dearer to clear, and there are more of you wanting it.

The teaching share I described above counts **every field you own, on every island, together**, and it counts the **highest island first**: Antura's fields take the best places, in their own order, and Taiar's follow behind them. So a second homestead never starts the share over. It sits above the first, and the farm screen tells you when another island's fields are counting ahead of the ones you are looking at.

I mention this because it surprised me. My dear old Novita fields, which had taught me everything, went quiet the week Farefield came up, and I felt obscurely betrayed by vegetables.

## Tending

Tend a field with a bucket and you shave a tenth off whatever growing time remains. Fifteen seconds a field, and across a large farmstead it accumulates into something that looks almost like cleverness.

It is the closest thing to a trick this trade permits, and I want you to notice how modest it is. Farming does not reward cleverness. Farming rewards *turning up*.

## Connections

Farming does not stand alone, and was never meant to.

- **Carpentry** builds everything you farm upon, the farmstead and every fence panel. Raise it alongside Farming, or buy from someone who did.
- **Mining** supplies the granite bordering every field.
- **Cooking** is where the grain goes, and the reason to grow more than you can eat.
- **Husbandry** shares your land and your manure, and each trade makes the other cheaper. Barley, beans, beet, and cabbage are all fodder as well as food.
- **Foraging** finds the seeds Antura's fields are sown from.

If you are wondering which to raise first: the Carpentry, then the farm. Do it the other way about and you will spend a memorable afternoon standing in Novita, waiting on planks, composing a document.
